Divergence from the plan above

The plan was right about the shape and wrong about the number of passes. Boss went to Inventory first, Inventory found 0 on hand and the vendor blocked by invoice 501, and that became an Accounting problem — exactly as predicted, including the depth-2 hop. The $840 payment was queued and nothing moved until it was approved.

What the plan did not anticipate is that it took three runs. The first ended correctly with the payment queued. After it was approved and the vendor hold lifted, the second run re-read the shop and still did not order the tee — it reported the shortfall and stopped. That gap was in the Inventory prompt, which said to request an order "once the path is clear" without insisting on it; the prompt was tightened to queue the order, do not stop at reporting the shortfall, and the third run queued the $8.00 purchase order immediately.

The plan also expected Customer Service to be called once. It was called on every pass, and each time it re-read stock itself and delegated to Inventory rather than trusting what it had been handed — Customer Service → Inventory appears twice. That is better behaviour than the plan asked for.

Three drafts accumulated across the three passes, each superseding the last. Only the final one reflects the placed order. A tidier system would supersede drafts rather than stack them; that is a real gap, and it is recorded here rather than hidden.

Divergence from the plan above

The plan was right about the route and badly wrong about reliability. Boss went to Facilities, Facilities verified $2,400 due 2026-09-02 against the shop's own date, and then delegated to Accounting to see what else was pulling on the account — the hop the plan called "the one that matters". All as predicted.

But Facilities ran twice without ever queueing the payment. Both times it read the lease, confirmed the amount was owed and affordable, drafted a reply about it, and handed back a report with an empty approval list. The analysis was right and the job was not done: the human was left to do the thing the agent had been asked to do. Its prompt already told it to queue; the prompt was not enough.

The fix was moved out of the prompt and into code. An @agent.output_validator on the two money agents now rejects any report that says a person needs to act while queueing nothing and naming nothing blocking it, with a ModelRetry telling it to queue or explain. On the third run the request appeared — and it came from Accounting, which Facilities had delegated to, not from Facilities itself. Either is legitimate; the team routed around its own weak spot.

The plan's low-confidence prediction — that Boss might also ask Customer Service to acknowledge the landlord — happened, on every pass. Two drafts for Elm City Properties are on the board.

Reflection

written after the run7 runs across 3 tickets167 tool calls346,241 tokens

Across the three Campus Customs tickets, the agent team showed both the value and the limits of specialization. It was strongest when a ticket genuinely crossed domains — stock, a vendor, the cash account, and something a customer had to be told. It was weakest when a ticket did not, and the structure cost more than it returned. Adding agents did not, on its own, produce better coordination.

1. Evaluating the agents' performance on each ticket

Ticket 101 — Bulldog tee, size S

This was the team's best work. Boss delegated to Inventory first, which called check_stock and found 0 on hand against 1 requested, then list_vendors and list_invoices and discovered the real obstacle: Bulldog Print Co was returning will_ship: false because invoice 501 for $840.00 was open and three days overdue. Inventory did not try to work around that. It delegated to Accounting, which confirmed the figure and queued approval #1.

The strength here was that no agent treated the first fact as the whole answer. "The tee is out of stock" is true and useless; "the tee is out of stock, the only apparel vendor will not ship, and the reason is an $840 bill" is a decision a person can act on. Customer Service made the same move in miniature — it called check_stock and list_purchase_orders itself rather than repeating what it had been handed, and it delegated back to Inventory twice to verify before writing.

The weakness was that it took three runs. The second pass, after the invoice was paid and the vendor was free, re-read everything and still did not order the tee. It reported the shortfall and stopped. That was a gap in the Inventory prompt, not in the reasoning.

Ticket 102 — Rent due

The analysis was correct every time and the work was not done twice. Facilities called check_rent_due and confirmed $2,400.00 due 2026-09-02, two days after the shop's own date rather than the real calendar — exactly the check the ticket needed, because a landlord's email is a claim until the lease record agrees with it. It then delegated to Accounting, which called list_invoices and get_cash_balance and surfaced the competing $840 claim that Facilities' own tools cannot see. That hop was genuinely useful.

And then Facilities handed back a report with an empty approval list. Twice. It had established that $2,400 was owed and affordable, drafted a reply saying payment was pending, and queued nothing — leaving me to do the thing it had been asked to do. This is the most important failure in the whole project, because the agent was not confused. It was right about every fact and still did not act, and its prompt already told it to.

What finally worked was not a better prompt. It was an @agent.output_validator on the two money agents that rejects any report claiming a person needs to act while queueing nothing and naming nothing blocking it. On the third run the request appeared — and it came from Accounting, which Facilities had delegated to, not from Facilities itself.

Ticket 103 — Bulk hoodie discount

The clearest test, and the only ticket resolved in a single run. Inventory found 8 on hand against 20 requested, a shortfall of 12. Accounting returned check_discount_margin: $22.00 cost against $58.00 list, 62.07% at list, break-even at $22.00. Inventory queued a $264.00 purchase order. Customer Service drafted a reply that separated the 8 available now from the 12 that were not ordered and named no delivery date, because there was no order to date from.

Two things stood out. The loop guard fired in a live run for the first time: Customer Service tried to delegate back to Boss, already up-chain, and was refused with would_loop before it cost a token. And the Boss reached the cash conclusion unprompted — its rationale states that the $264 order "exceeds available uncommitted cash" of $160.00. Nobody asked it to check that.

Overall

I would evaluate the team as mixed, and more trustworthy than it was efficient. All three tickets resolved, but resolution alone is a weak measure — a ticket can be closed badly. The measures that mattered were whether the agents used the right tools, whether they invented anything, and whether the safety boundary held. On the last two the team was clean: every figure in every draft traces to a tool call in audit_trail.json, and across 167 tool calls no agent moved a cent. On efficiency it was poor: 346,241 tokens and seven runs for three tickets, and two of those runs produced analysis with no action attached.

2. Actual compared with Expected

Ticket 101 — the plan was right about the shape and wrong about the cost. I predicted Boss → Inventory first, because the first unknown is whether the tee is on the shelf, and that is what happened. I predicted the depth-2 hop to Accounting once the blocker turned out to be money, and that happened. I predicted that request_purchase_order_approval would be refused while the vendor was blocked, and it was. What I did not predict was three runs, or that Customer Service would be called on every pass and leave three drafts stacked against the same ticket, each superseding the last. Only the final one is true.

Ticket 102 — the route matched, the reliability did not. I predicted Boss → Facilities and called the Facilities → Accounting hop "the one that matters"; both happened on every pass. My low-confidence prediction — that Boss might also ask Customer Service to acknowledge the landlord even though a landlord is not a customer — also happened, every time. What the plan assumed without stating it was that an agent asked to prepare a payment would prepare one. That assumption was wrong twice, and it is the one I would never have found by reading the code.

Ticket 103 — the plan's first call was wrong and the run was right. I committed to Boss → Accounting first, reasoning that the club asked a pricing question and deserved a pricing answer. Boss went to Inventory. That is the better call: it establishes within one hop that the shop cannot supply 20 at any price, which is the fact that shapes every sentence of the reply. I had already written Inventory-first into the tab as a defensible alternative. I should have committed to it.

The pattern across all three is that my plans were more linear than the runs. I expected delegate → investigate → recommend → resolve. What happened was agents re-verifying each other's facts, re-reading the board between passes, and occasionally needing a second or third attempt. The Expected sections were still worth writing — without them I would have read the 103 run as obviously correct instead of noticing it had overruled me.

3. What would have been simpler with one agent and tools

Ticket 102 should not have needed a team. Paying rent is: read one lease row, compare one date against desk.date_today, read one balance, queue one payment. A single agent holding check_rent_due, get_cash_balance and request_payment_approval could have done it in about four tool calls. The team spent 54 tool calls, 10 delegation hops, three runs and 123,009 tokens on it — and the thing that finally made it work was a code-level validator, not another agent.

One agent would also have been easier to supervise. Instead of following four streams of delegation to find out why nothing was queued, I would have had one reasoning trace to read.

But I want to be careful not to overclaim, because the Facilities → Accounting hop on that same ticket did earn its place: it surfaced invoice 501, which Facilities' tools cannot see at all. A single rent-focused agent would have queued $2,400 against a $3,400 balance without knowing $840 was already overdue.

Ticket 101 is the case for the team, and the reason is not that five agents thought better than one would have. A single agent holding all eighteen tools could have reached the same answer. The reason is separation of duties and legibility. Customer Service physically cannot call record_payment — it is not in its toolset — so a drafting agent can never touch the cash account no matter what it is asked. And the audit trail reads as five distinguishable voices, which is what let me find the Facilities failure in minutes rather than scrolling one long monologue.

So the rule I would take from this is not "one agent" or "a team". It is: use one agent when the work is narrow and the tools are safe to hold together; use a team when the value is in keeping certain tools apart, or when an independent check reduces a real error.

4. Three new problems this team could solve with the tools I built

Restocking the Crest Mug. CC-MUG-CREST is sitting at 0 units in Aisle D — the same condition that made ticket 101 a problem, on a product nobody has complained about yet. Inventory would confirm the zero with check_stock, read list_vendors and find Elm City Gifts (specialty "mugs and small goods", 3 lead days, no open invoice, will_ship: true), and queue a purchase order at the $3.50 unit cost. Accounting would check it against the $160.00 left in checking. Every tool needed already exists.

A bulk order the shop can actually fill. If a department asked for 30 CC-HAT-BLUE caps, Inventory would confirm 40 on hand — fillable from stock, no vendor, no lead time — and Accounting would price the discount against the $6.00 cost and $22.00 list using check_discount_margin. Customer Service would draft the quote. This ticket would move no money at all, which makes it the cleanest fit of the three: the team's whole job would be establishing facts and writing a truthful reply.

A rent increase at renewal. If Elm City Properties proposed raising the Chapel Street rent, Facilities would read the current $2,400.00 from check_rent_due, Accounting would test the new figure against get_cash_balance and the margins in pricing, Boss would decide whether it is affordable given that no revenue is modelled, and Customer Service would draft the reply to the landlord. This is the same shape as ticket 102, which the team has already proved it can route.

All three fit because they involve structured records the MCP server already reads, and any money that moves still passes through the approval queue.

5. Three new problems it could not solve, and what would be needed

Selling anything. Ticket 101 is a customer order the shop can never actually fulfil, because there is no orders table, no way to decrement inventory on a sale, and no revenue path — cash only goes out in this model, so the balance can only ever fall from $160.00. The team can promise a tee it has no mechanism to hand over. Needed: an orders table, a fulfil_order tool that decrements stock inside a transaction, a receive_payment tool that credits cash_accounts, and a sales or fulfilment agent that owns the order lifecycle.

Receiving the delivery it just ordered. Purchase order #1 is recorded as arriving 2026-09-05, but place_purchase_order deliberately never touches inventory, there is no receive_purchase_order tool, and desk.date_today is frozen at 2026-08-31 with no tool that can advance it. The shop can order goods and can never take them in. Needed: a receiving tool that increments stock and closes the order, a tool to advance the shop clock, a vendor-invoice ingestion path so the $8.00 eventually becomes a payable, and a receiving agent — or an extension of Inventory's scope.

Chasing Bulldog Print Co when the tee does not arrive. Every message this team writes stops at message_drafts with status on_board, by design. There is no outbound path, no inbound one, and no vendor-facing tool at all, so the team cannot ask where the order is or negotiate a shorter lead time. It can only draft a note to a person who would have to send it. Needed: a send tool with its own human approval gate (the same pattern as payments, since contacting a vendor is also consequential), an inbound message store so replies become tickets, and a vendor-relations agent that owns the thread.

These limits matter because they show the team's reach is set by its tools, not by its reasoning. On ticket 103 the Boss correctly identified an affordability problem it had no tool to prevent — it could see the exposure and not stop it. A system like this should escalate at exactly that boundary rather than act as though the boundary is not there.

Conclusion

This project turned three one-line tickets into a visible, auditable workflow. The dashboard showed which agent was working, what it delegated, which tool it called and what came back, and the Cash tab forced the harder question — not whether a ticket was closed, but whether the money was right. It was: $3,400.00 → $160.00, matching cash_accounts exactly, with both payments stamped with a human name.

My main takeaway is that a rule that matters belongs in the tool, not in the instructions. Every guardrail that held was structural: decide_approval is in no agent's toolset, so no agent could approve anything; the vendor hold is checked in the tool, so ticket 101 simply could not order while invoice 501 was open; the loop guard is code, so Customer Service's circular delegation never reached the model. Every rule that failed was a sentence in a prompt. Facilities was told to queue the payment and did not, twice, and the fix was a validator rather than better wording.

That principle also names the roadmap. Coordination cost is the design's weakest point — seven runs, stacked drafts, every pass starting from scratch — and one guardrail is still only half structural: the payment tool refuses to overdraw the account, but the order tool has no equivalent check, which is why a $264 commitment against $160 of cash was caught by the Boss's judgement and my click rather than by a rule. Both are tractable, and both are the same kind of work as the fixes that already landed.

In the same way that my earlier team experiences taught me that division of labour only helps when someone plans for reintegration, this project taught me that delegation only helps when the system has a structural way to bring the work back — and that the point is not to maximise the number of agents, but to build enough structure that each one can contribute while the whole process stays understandable, efficient and safe.
